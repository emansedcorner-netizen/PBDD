You don’t start raising a strong, unapologetic Muslim child at 15.

You start forming them in the early years.

The goal isn’t to spend the next 18 years managing your child. It’s to spend the early years forming the foundations that eventually help them lead themselves.

You are building their faith, identity, character, habits, relationship with Allah, relationship with you, and the way they understand themselves and the world around them.

When those foundations are clear, you do not have to parent every new situation from scratch. You have a direction to return to.

Do less. Lead better.
THE REAL PROBLEM

The problem is not that you don't have more information.

You are probably already reading, listening, researching, correcting, teaching, worrying, making du'a, and trying to make the best decisions you can for your child.

The problem is that most parents are parenting one situation at a time.

What do I do about screens?
What do I say about Halloween?
How do I get her to pray?
What consequence should I give?
How do I make him proud to be Muslim?
How do I stop yelling?

Each answer becomes another piece of information to remember. Another strategy. Another voice in your head.

Your child does not need 100 disconnected parenting answers.

They need you to understand the foundations underneath all of them.

Because once you know what you are forming in the first 12 years, the thousands of little parenting decisions become much easier to make.

That is what Parenting by Divine Design teaches you.

Picture this instead

Imagine knowing what you are doing.

Your 6-year-old asks why her friends celebrate something you do not, and you know how to answer her.

Your 8-year-old pushes against a boundary, and you do not immediately wonder whether you are being too strict.

Your child begins salah, and you understand what this stage is actually for instead of expecting the finished habit overnight.

Your child starts approaching puberty, and you know how to prepare them before the physical, emotional, and religious changes arrive instead of scrambling once they are already in it.

You hit a hard phase and catch yourself yelling more than usual, and instead of wondering whether what your child is going through is developmentally normal, you understand what is happening and know how to respond.

Screen time feels like a constant tug-of-war. You need the screen for your own mental health, and instead of carrying the guilt alone, you have support and a clear plan for how screens fit into your home.

Something concerning appears at school, online, or in the culture and instead of running to Instagram asking, “What do Muslim parents do about this?” you know how to think it through.

You know exactly whether or not you want to home educate, and why, and you still have a home curriculum that supports whichever education you choose, because you know how much that matters in the early years.

You know your principles. You understand your child.
You know what your family stands for.

And your children are growing up inside that certainty.

ASMA'S STORY

When the “why” questions started getting harder.

When Asma's 7-year-old asked her why they don't celebrate Halloween, she froze.

She wanted to answer in a way that protected her daughter's fitrah and identity, but every explanation she tried felt clunky, defensive, or… guilt-inducing.

“I didn't want my child to feel like being Muslim meant always saying ‘no.’ But I also didn't know how to explain our deen in a way she could actually understand.”

For months, she avoided the questions, hoping her daughter wouldn't notice. But the questions only got harder:

  • “Why can't I get Christmas presents like my friends?”
  • “Why can't we decorate our house like theirs?”
  • “Why can't I just be like them?”

Asma joined Forming Early Foundations (FEF) out of desperation, hoping to find better words.

“I didn't expect what I found… FEF didn't just give me answers. It gave me a framework to explain Islam in a way that's positive, loving, and connected to Allah, not defensive or fear-based.”

Now, instead of panicking when her daughter asks why, Asma smiles. She knows exactly how to explain deen with confidence and simplicity.

Her daughter doesn't feel deprived anymore. She feels proud.

“I didn't realize how much my uncertainty was shaping her identity. Now, alhamdulillah, she understands what we believe — and why — without me feeling like the ‘fun police.’ That's priceless.”

Stories are based on member experiences shared with The Good Child. Some identifying details may be summarized or anonymized.

THE PBDD FRAMEWORK

We build the foundations in 3 places.

You. Your child. Your home.

01

You

Who you are matters. Your education, obligations, habits, reactions, strengths, weak spots, expectations, and the way you lead.

Become more skilled and intentional instead of rebuilding your parenting every time a new problem appears.

02

Your Child

Understand their development, temperament, behavior, needs, emotional world, and Islamic identity.

Know what the child in front of you needs now, not what an imaginary “ideal child” should need.

03

Your Home

Your home is the third parent. Build the values, routines, boundaries, relationships, Islamic habits, and culture your child grows inside.

Raising a strong Muslim child is not one lesson. It is the life you build around them.

WHY LEARN THIS FROM ME?

Salam, I’m Eman.

I’m a mom of five, all 13 and under, with a BAsc (Honours) degree in Child Studies and Early Education.

For more than a decade, I’ve worked with children and families as a teacher, early interventionist, resource consultant, and early years program supervisor. Today, I’m raising and home educating my own children while bringing that field experience and expertise to Muslim families online.
